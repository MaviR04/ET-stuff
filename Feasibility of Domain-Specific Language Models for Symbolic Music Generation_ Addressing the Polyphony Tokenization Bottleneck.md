# **1\. Introduction**

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence in the audio domain has yielded highly capable generalist models that generate acoustic waveforms directly from natural language prompts. While these models are proficient at broad text-to-audio tasks, they exhibit significant limitations when tasked with precise, structural musical generation. Generalist architectures struggle to capture fine-grained genre characteristics, control specific musical attributes, or represent culturally diverse music accurately. The semantic disconnect between the continuous, high-dimensional acoustic space of audio waveforms and the discrete, hierarchical logic of music theory limits the degree to which these models can manipulate the compositional rules governing harmony, melody, and rhythm.

In response, the field of Music Information Retrieval (MIR) has increasingly turned to Domain-Specific Language Models (DSLMs) tailored to symbolic music. Symbolic music generation represents music as a sequence of symbols, followed by feature learning and generative modeling [1]. A symbolic Music DSLM applies Transformer-based language modeling directly to the structural code of music, using discrete formats such as MIDI, MusicXML, or ABC notation. By treating music structurally, much as text is treated in Natural Language Processing (NLP), these models trade the generic, uneditable output of waveform generation for precision and editability. Generation tasks in this domain are commonly categorized into five types: generation from scratch, conditional generation, controllable generation, performance generation, and interactive generation [1].

This paper reviews the literature on Transformer-based symbolic music generation, with particular attention to how music is serialized into token sequences. Unlike natural language, which is almost exclusively sequential, music is multi-dimensional: it combines vertical harmonization, horizontal counterpoint, dynamic expression, and precise metrical timing, and any tokenization must decide how to project that structure onto a one-dimensional sequence. The review proceeds in three parts. Section 2.1 surveys the architectural foundations of the field. Section 2.2 examines three design tensions that recur across tokenization schemes and describes how the literature has responded to each, arguing that these are trade-offs the field now navigates competently rather than unsolved problems. Section 2.3 identifies four questions that remain genuinely open, and argues that the binding constraints on symbolic DSLMs are no longer architectural but evaluative and empirical.

# **2\. Transformer-Based Symbolic Music Generation**

## **2.1 Architectural Foundations**

Current literature on symbolic music generation predominantly revolves around adapting the Transformer architecture to model discrete musical sequences. Early neural sequence models relied on n-gram Markov models, Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. While LSTMs succeeded at specific counting tasks, they lacked the inductive biases required to model the self-referential structures of music over extended durations [1].

### **2.1.1 Foundational Tokenization Frameworks**

To adapt NLP models to music, a score must be serialized. Early foundational work established the "MIDI-like" event-based encoding, which flattens a score into a 1D token sequence [2]. A standard MIDI-like vocabulary uses a 388-token space comprising four event types: note-on, note-off, set-velocity, and time-shift [2], [3]. The time-shift event compresses sustained note states into a single event, dictating shifts ranging from 10 to 1000 milliseconds [2]. Alternatively, grid-based encodings such as REMI place events on a strict metrical grid using absolute bar and beat position tokens, replacing relative time-shifts in order to stabilize rhythm [4].

### **2.1.2 Attention and Long-Range Structure**

A persistent challenge in adopting Transformers for music is the computational cost of attention over long sequences: the time and space complexity of standard self-attention grows quadratically with sequence length, ![][image1], where ![][image2] is the sequence length and ![][image3] is the embedding dimension [3]. Musical regularity, moreover, depends on relative timing, distance, and periodicity rather than absolute position. The Music Transformer introduced an optimized relative self-attention mechanism that modulates attention weights by pairwise token distance, reducing the intermediate memory required for relative information from ![][image1] to linear ![][image4] complexity and demonstrating minute-long piano compositions with coherent long-term structure [3]. Subsequent models extended the effective context through hierarchical and coarse-grained attention: the Bar Transformer introduced a note-to-bar encoder hierarchy [5], while Museformer combined fine-grained attention to structurally relevant bars with coarse-grained summaries of the remainder, modelling sequences several times longer than a full-attention counterpart [6].

### **2.1.3 Multi-Track Paradigms**

Multi-track models must decide how to arrange several concurrent instrumental streams into one sequence. The conventional approach interleaves events chronologically, merging all instruments into a unified timeline. The Multi-Track Music Machine (MMM) instead proposed non-chronological track concatenation: rather than interleaving at the event level, it builds a complete time-ordered sequence for each track and concatenates these macroscopic tracks end to end [7]. Because self-attention establishes unmediated connections between any two positions regardless of distance, this arrangement does not sacrifice cross-track coherence, and it enables direct user control over track-level and bar-level inpainting, instrumentation, and note density [7]. Later work such as the Multitrack Music Transformer retained explicit instrument identity within a compact multi-dimensional token space, showing that track-aware representations can shorten sequences while preserving controllability over instrumentation [8].

### **2.1.4 Text-Native Formats and Natural Language Conditioning**

As the field has coalesced around pre-trained Large Language Models (LLMs), researchers have explored text-based symbolic formats, principally ABC notation [9]. ABC encodes scores using ASCII characters, representing pitch, rhythm, and articulation in a compressed, human-readable string [9]. Empirical findings indicate that LLMs are more compatible with ABC notation than with binary-derived MIDI formats, since ABC aligns with the textual reasoning already embedded in their pre-trained weights [10].

A parallel development is the generation of symbolic music from free-form natural language. Datasets such as MetaScore pair a large collection of scores with metadata scraped from an online forum, then use a pre-trained LLM to translate those tags into pseudo-natural-language captions [11]. Building on similar data, text2midi uses a pre-trained LLM encoder to process a prompt and condition an autoregressive Transformer decoder, bridging semantic text comprehension and structural MIDI generation [12].

## **2.2 Design Tensions and Current Responses**

The literature surfaces three recurring tensions in the representation of symbolic music. Each is real, and each has shaped a decade of design decisions; none, however, remains unsolved. This section states each tension and the response the field has converged on.

### **2.2.1 Metrical Drift versus Token Bloat**

The first tension concerns how time is encoded. Event-based frameworks use relative time-shift tokens, which model expressive performance well but carry no explicit notion of beat or downbeat. This admits *metrical drift*: if a time-shift is generated at the wrong value, the alignment of all subsequent notes is affected, and rhythm degrades [2]. Grid-based encodings such as REMI eliminate drift by anchoring events to absolute bar and beat positions, but require several positional tokens per musical event, inflating sequence length and consuming context window [4].

The response has been to stop treating this as a binary choice. Compound Word (CP) representations group the tokens belonging to a single musical event — pitch, velocity, duration, instrument — into one compound token occupying a single sequence position, so that metrical anchoring no longer costs sequence length [13]. The Octuple encoding used by MusicBERT applies the same principle with a fixed eight-element tuple per note [14]. Both preserve the internal simultaneity of a musical event while substantially shortening the sequence, which dissolves the trade-off rather than resolving it in one direction.

### **2.2.2 Serializing Polyphony: Ordering and Compression**

The second tension is more specific to music than to text. Transformers consume one-dimensional sequences, but a polyphonic score is two-dimensional, and simultaneous notes have no canonical order. A tokenizer must therefore impose an arbitrary ordering convention on notes that sound together, and the model expends capacity learning a convention that carries no musical meaning. This, rather than any failure of attention over distance, is the substantive difficulty in serializing polyphony.

Compound representations again mitigate the problem by reducing the number of positions over which an arbitrary order must be imposed [13], [14]. Refinements target the internal structure of the compound token itself: a Feature Interaction Fusion module facilitates intra-group feature interaction to improve input representation before sequence modeling [15], and an N-gram Transformer encoder exploits the co-occurrence regularities of adjacent musical events, treating local patterns as N-grams to capture repetition [16].

A second line of work compresses sequences statistically. Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), implemented in libraries such as MidiTok [17], iteratively merges the most frequent pairs of musical tokens into "supertokens," compressing average sequence lengths by up to 50% and improving downstream performance [18]. BPE's behaviour is, however, strongly dependent on instrumentation. Because simultaneous notes can be ordered in many ways when flattened, BPE requires over ten times as many merge steps on a polyphonic piano corpus to reach an average supertoken length comparable to that obtained on a monophonic corpus [19].

Notably, the resulting supertokens are not musically arbitrary. Analysis of the segmentation induced by a 1024-merge BPE found that only 4.2% of supertokens straddle a phrase boundary, against a 71% overlap ratio when a piece is split randomly into the same number of chunks, indicating that supertokens are in fact unlikely to span across phrases; the same study reports that BPE improves performance on a musical phrase segmentation task in the polyphonic setting, while benefiting monophonic tunes only within a specific range of merges [19]. The obstacle for dense polyphony is therefore one of compression efficiency — assembling supertokens of useful length — rather than of semantic misalignment between statistical compression and musical structure.

### **2.2.3 Autoregressive Decoding and Degeneration**

The third tension concerns generation rather than representation. Autoregressive models are trained by teacher forcing to minimize the negative log-likelihood of an observed sequence, but at inference must condition on their own outputs. The standard account holds that minor errors compound recursively, shifting the generated distribution away from the training distribution — *exposure bias* — and that in symbolic music this manifests as extreme repetitiveness, near-empty sections, or discordant leaps between phrases [20]. Transformer-GAN frameworks address this by adding an adversarial loss that penalizes precisely these artifacts, and report that 57% of human listeners prefer the adversarial output over a likelihood-maximization baseline [20].

This diagnosis warrants qualification. Work in NLP has argued that degenerate repetition is substantially an artifact of *decoding* rather than of the training objective: maximization-based strategies such as beam search produce repetitive, bland text from models whose distributions are otherwise well-calibrated, and truncated stochastic sampling largely removes the pathology without retraining [21]. Since much of the symbolic music literature reports results under greedy or beam decoding, the extent to which musical degeneration reflects exposure bias as opposed to decoding strategy is not firmly established, and claims attributing repetition to the training objective should be read with that ambiguity in mind.

A structural alternative avoids the question. The discrete symbolic domain can be parameterized into a continuous latent space using a pre-trained Variational Autoencoder such as MusicVAE [22], after which a denoising diffusion probabilistic model refines latent embeddings through an iterative, non-autoregressive process, eliminating teacher forcing entirely [23]. This approach reports strong results against autoregressive baselines, though the original demonstration operates on short, largely monophonic latent segments, and scaling it to full polyphonic textures remains an open engineering problem rather than a settled result.

## **2.3 Open Problems**

The tensions above are navigable with existing methods. Four questions are not, and they constrain the field more tightly than any remaining architectural difficulty.

### **2.3.1 Evaluation**

Symbolic music generation lacks an agreed method for measuring the quality it claims to produce. Subjective listening tests are small, expensive, and difficult to reproduce, and the fuzzy definition of musical creativity combined with varying system goals has made subjective evaluation appear to be the only viable methodology [24]. Objective metrics — pitch-class histograms, groove consistency, chord progression statistics — are computable but correlate imperfectly with perceived musicality. A detailed analysis of one Transformer-based system found a clear gap between listener ratings of generated and human compositions even where training loss was driven low, and required a battery of complementary measures, including fitness scape plots for structureness, to characterize where the model actually failed [25]. Because comparative claims throughout this literature depend on evaluation, this gap propagates: assertions that one architecture "significantly outperforms" another inherit the uncertainty of the instrument used to measure them.

### **2.3.2 Data Scale, Provenance, and the Limits of the Scaling Argument**

The most prominent recent claim in the field is that symbolic music obeys a scaling law analogous to those observed in NLP, with performance improving predictably in both parameter count and training data [10]. The claim is empirically supported within the range tested, but its practical reach is bounded by data that may not exist. General-purpose symbolic corpora remain orders of magnitude smaller than text corpora: the Lakh MIDI Dataset, long the field's largest general collection, contains on the order of 10⁵ files [26], and MetaScore, among the largest recent efforts, comprises roughly 963,000 scores [11]. Provenance compounds scarcity, since large symbolic collections are typically scraped from user-contributed repositories whose licensing is inconsistent and often unclear.

A scaling law is only actionable to the extent that the corpus can grow along the axis it describes. Establishing whether symbolic music can supply data at the volumes the law implies — and on what legal basis — is a prerequisite for the scaling argument rather than a corollary of it, and it is largely unaddressed.

### **2.3.3 Structure at Song Scale**

Extending context windows is not the same as generating coherent long-form music. Attention mechanisms now reach sequence lengths covering entire pieces [6], [10], yet the compositional problem of song-scale organization — establishing a theme, developing it, returning to it in varied form, and marking sectional boundaries — is distinct from the problem of attending over a long span. Hierarchical approaches that generate structure before content, such as cascaded diffusion over whole-song representations, treat form as an explicit object of modeling rather than an emergent property of a long context [27]. Whether coherent large-scale form is best obtained by explicit hierarchy, by scale, or by some combination remains unresolved.

### **2.3.4 Voice and Part Identity**

Most widely used tokenizations discard the information that a note belongs to a particular voice or part. MIDI channels approximate instrumentation but not the voice-leading structure that governs counterpoint, and once notes are flattened into a shared stream, the identity of individual lines is recoverable only by inference. This limits idiomatic writing for ensembles and makes contrapuntal evaluation difficult. Track-aware representations retain instrument identity [8], and multimodal work that pairs MIDI with score images recovers notation-level information — staff assignment, voicing, articulation — that symbolic formats alone discard, by segmenting score images into bar-aligned patches with Vision Transformers and aligning them to CP-based MIDI representations through masked bar-attribute modeling [28]. Neither line yet delivers a representation in which voice is a first-class, model-visible entity.

# **3\. Conclusion**

This review has surveyed Transformer-based symbolic music generation, distinguishing the design tensions the field has learned to navigate from the questions that remain open. The recurring trade-offs of tokenization — metrical stability against sequence length, and the arbitrary ordering imposed when polyphony is flattened — have been substantially addressed by compound and grouped representations, by statistical compression whose segmentations align better with musical phrasing than a purely compression-oriented account would predict, and by non-autoregressive alternatives that sidestep teacher forcing altogether. Attention is no longer the binding constraint on long-form symbolic generation, and neither, on current evidence, is tokenization.

What constrains the field instead is measurement and data. Without an evaluation methodology that reliably tracks musical quality, comparative architectural claims rest on an unstable foundation, and the reported superiority of any given approach is difficult to verify. Without symbolic corpora that can grow by orders of magnitude on a clear legal basis, the scaling laws now invoked to justify large symbolic models cannot be exercised to their stated limits. Progress on song-scale form and on voice-aware representation is likely to depend on both. Future work that establishes shared evaluation protocols and well-licensed corpora at scale would do more to advance symbolic Music DSLMs than further refinement of the tokenization schemes this review has surveyed.
